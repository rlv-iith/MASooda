From arrays to ADMM: every equation in MASooda, explained

This guide starts from what an array is and builds up to every equation the simulator uses: separation, consensus ADMM, resilient consensus, signal temporal logic, the battery circuit and the Kalman filter. Each idea comes with a picture, a worked example with real numbers from the code, the rule it obeys, and the exact function in the repository that computes it.

Numbers in the worked examples were computed with the repository's own functions on branch bms/publishable (configuration configs/base_config.yaml). Where the code and an equation differ in a detail, the code wins and the text says so.

1Arrays, vectors and matrices

The whole simulator is arithmetic on arrays. This chapter builds every tool the later chapters use.

1.1 Arrays and their shape

An array is a grid of numbers with a shape. NumPy, the library MASooda uses, writes the shape as a tuple:

  • shape (2,): one position \((x, y)\);
  • shape (N, 2): the positions of \(N\) drones, one row each;
  • shape (T, N, D): a trajectory, \(T\) time samples × \(N\) drones × \(D\) coordinates. The STL monitor reads exactly this shape.

1.2 Vectors: adding, scaling, length

A vector is an arrow. Adding vectors puts arrows head to tail; multiplying by a number stretches the arrow.

\[ \mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}=(a_x+b_x,\ a_y+b_y),\qquad c\,\mathbf{a}=(c\,a_x,\ c\,a_y),\qquad \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert=\sqrt{a_x^2+a_y^2}. \]

The length \(\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\) is Pythagoras. The distance between two drones is the length of their difference:

\[ d_{ij}=\lVert\mathbf{p}_i-\mathbf{p}_j\rVert . \]

A unit vector has length 1 and only carries a direction: \(\hat{\mathbf{a}}=\mathbf{a}/\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\).

Rule · clamp a vectorTo keep a speed or acceleration under a limit \(m\) without changing its direction, rescale it when it is too long: \(\mathbf{v}\leftarrow \mathbf{v}\cdot \min\!\big(1,\ m/\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert\big)\).
In codesimulation/dynamics.py › clamp_vector_2d
ExampleA command \(\mathbf{v}=(12,\,9)\) m/s has length \(\sqrt{144+81}=15\) m/s. With the 15 m/s limit it passes unchanged. The command \((16,\,12)\) has length 20 and is scaled by \(15/20=0.75\) to \((12,\,9)\).

1.3 The dot product, projection and the "left" vector

The dot product multiplies matching entries and adds them:

\[ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=a_xb_x+a_yb_y=\lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert\cos\theta . \]

If \(\hat{\mathbf{t}}\) is a unit vector, \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{t}}\) is how far \(\mathbf{a}\) reaches along \(\hat{\mathbf{t}}\). MASooda uses this constantly: "along-track" distance to the drone ahead is \(\mathbf{r}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{t}}\), and the sideways ("lateral") offset is \(\mathbf{r}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\), where \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) is the unit vector to the left of the direction of travel:

\[ \hat{\mathbf{t}}=(t_x,t_y)\quad\Rightarrow\quad \hat{\mathbf{n}}_{\text{left}}=(-t_y,\ t_x). \]
ExampleI fly east, \(\hat{\mathbf{t}}=(1,0)\), so \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}=(0,1)\). A drone sits at \(\mathbf{r}=(30,4)\) m from me. Along-track \(=30\cdot1+4\cdot0=30\) m; lateral \(=30\cdot0+4\cdot1=4\) m. It is 30 m ahead, 4 m to my left, inside my 20 m tube (half-width 10 m), so it counts as traffic ahead.

1.4 Matrices

A matrix is a table of numbers that acts on vectors. Multiplying matrix \(M\) by vector \(\mathbf{v}\) takes the dot product of each row with \(\mathbf{v}\):

\[ M\mathbf{v}=\begin{pmatrix} m_{11} & m_{12}\\ m_{21} & m_{22}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v_1\\ v_2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix} m_{11}v_1+m_{12}v_2\\ m_{21}v_1+m_{22}v_2\end{pmatrix}. \]
  • The transpose \(M^\top\) swaps rows and columns. A matrix with \(M=M^\top\) is symmetric; adjacency matrices, Laplacians and covariance matrices all are.
  • The identity \(I\) has ones on the diagonal and leaves vectors unchanged: \(I\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}\).
  • The trace \(\operatorname{tr}(M)\) is the sum of the diagonal.
  • An eigenvalue \(\lambda\) and eigenvector \(\mathbf{u}\) satisfy \(M\mathbf{u}=\lambda\mathbf{u}\): the matrix only stretches \(\mathbf{u}\). A symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues; np.linalg.eigvalsh computes them. Chapter 4 uses the second-smallest eigenvalue of the graph Laplacian.

1.5 All pairwise distances at once: broadcasting

Many functions need every distance \(d_{ij}\) between \(N\) drones, an \(N\times N\) matrix. NumPy builds it without loops by broadcasting: subtract a (N,1,2) copy from a (1,N,2) copy to get every difference in a (N,N,2) array, then take lengths along the last axis.

deltas    = positions[:, None, :] - positions[None, :, :]   # (N, N, 2)
distances = np.sqrt(np.sum(deltas * deltas, axis=2))        # (N, N)
ExampleThree drones at \((0,0)\), \((12,5)\), \((30,0)\): \[ D=\begin{pmatrix}0 & 13 & 30\\ 13 & 0 & 18.68\\ 30 & 18.68 & 0\end{pmatrix}\ \text{m}. \] \(d_{12}=\sqrt{12^2+5^2}=13\); \(d_{23}=\sqrt{18^2+5^2}=18.68\). With the 20 m ADMM detection radius, pairs (1,2) and (2,3) are in conflict and (1,3) is not. Section 4.4 uses this example again.

2Motion of one drone

Every drone is a point that obeys a double integrator: acceleration changes velocity, velocity changes position.

2.1 The double integrator and Euler steps

Continuous motion is \(\dot{\mathbf{p}}=\mathbf{v}\), \(\dot{\mathbf{v}}=\mathbf{a}\). The simulator advances in small steps \(\Delta t=0.1\) s (forward Euler):

\[ \mathbf{p}_{t+1}=\mathbf{p}_t+\mathbf{v}_t\,\Delta t,\qquad \mathbf{v}_{t+1}=\operatorname{clamp}\!\big(\mathbf{v}_t+\operatorname{clamp}(\mathbf{a}_t,a_{\max})\,\Delta t,\ v_{\max}\big), \]

with \(v_{\max}=15\) m/s. Altitude is not integrated: a drone sits on a discrete layer \(L\in\{0,\dots,6\}\) whose height is \(30+15L\) m.

2.2 Tracking a desired velocity

The higher layers (local rules, ADMM) produce a desired velocity \(\mathbf{v}^{\text{des}}\). The engine turns it into an acceleration with a first-order lag of time constant \(\tau_v=0.5\) s:

\[ \mathbf{a}=\frac{\mathbf{v}^{\text{des}}-\mathbf{v}}{\tau_v}. \]

The gap between actual and desired velocity shrinks by a factor \(e^{-\Delta t/\tau_v}\) per step, roughly 18% per tick.

ExampleFlying \(\mathbf{v}=(10,0)\), desired \((12,0)\): \(\mathbf{a}=(2,0)/0.5=(4,0)\) m/s². After one tick \(\mathbf{v}=(10.4,0)\) and the drone moved \(10\times0.1=1\) m.
In codesimulation/dynamics.py › step_dynamicssimulation/route_engine.py › _simulate (accel = (desired − velocity) / tau_v)

3Corridors, maps and routes

Roads become a graph, routes become polylines, and the corridor tube is a distance rule around each polyline.

3.1 From map to local metres

Map coordinates are longitude \(\lambda\) and latitude \(\varphi\) in degrees. Over a few kilometres the Earth is flat enough for a local tangent plane around a reference point \((\lambda_0,\varphi_0)\). A degree of latitude is about 111 km everywhere; a degree of longitude shrinks with \(\cos\varphi_0\):

\[ x=(\lambda-\lambda_0)\,m_{\text{lon}}\cos\varphi_0,\qquad y=(\varphi-\varphi_0)\,m_{\text{lat}},\qquad m_{\text{lon}}\approx m_{\text{lat}}\approx1.11\times10^5\ \text{m per degree}. \]

At Hyderabad (\(\varphi_0\approx17.6^\circ\)), \(\cos\varphi_0\approx0.953\), so a degree of longitude is about 106 km. The inverse, to_geo, divides by the same factors.

3.2 Shortest paths: Dijkstra

Junctions are nodes, road pieces are edges weighted by length. A route between two vertiports is the shortest path. Dijkstra's algorithm keeps a priority queue of tentative distances and repeatedly settles the closest unsettled node:

\[ \text{dist}(v)\leftarrow\min\big(\text{dist}(v),\ \text{dist}(u)+w(u,v)\big)\quad\text{for each edge }(u,v). \]

On the campus graph (105 junctions) this takes well under a millisecond; on the Hyderabad graph (1,533 junctions) a few milliseconds.

3.3 A route as an arc-length polyline

A route is a list of points \(\mathbf{q}_0,\dots,\mathbf{q}_K\). Each segment has length \(\ell_k=\lVert\mathbf{q}_{k+1}-\mathbf{q}_k\rVert\) and unit direction \(\hat{\mathbf{t}}_k\). The cumulative length \(c_k=\sum_{m\lt k}\ell_m\) lets any distance \(s\) along the route map to a point:

\[ \mathbf{q}(s)=\mathbf{q}_k+\hat{\mathbf{t}}_k\,(s-c_k)\quad\text{for }c_k\le s\lt c_{k+1}. \]

3.4 Nearest point on a segment and the lateral error

To find where a drone at \(\mathbf{p}\) is along its route, project onto each nearby segment, clip to the segment, and keep the closest:

\[ \tau_k=\operatorname{clip}\big((\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}_k)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{t}}_k,\ 0,\ \ell_k\big),\qquad \mathbf{q}^\ast=\mathbf{q}_k+\tau_k\hat{\mathbf{t}}_k,\qquad e=(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}^\ast)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}. \]

\(e\) is the signed lateral error: positive to the left of the centreline. The search is limited to a window \([s-40,\ s+120]\) m around the last known progress so a route that crosses itself cannot make the drone "jump".

Rule · tube boundaryIf \(\lvert e\rvert\gt w/2\) (10 m), set \(\mathbf{p}\leftarrow\mathbf{q}^\ast+\operatorname{sign}(e)\,\tfrac{w}{2}\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) and remove the outward velocity: \(\mathbf{v}\leftarrow\mathbf{v}-\max(0,\mathbf{v}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{w}})\,\hat{\mathbf{w}}\), where \(\hat{\mathbf{w}}=\operatorname{sign}(e)\hat{\mathbf{n}}\). The map edge is a geofence handled the same way.

3.5 Pure-pursuit steering with a keep-left lane

A drone steers towards a point 40 m ahead on its route, shifted left by a lane offset (a quarter of the tube width, 5 m; India keeps left). With reference speed \(v_{\text{ref}}\) and lateral gain \(k_{\text{lat}}=0.5\ \text{s}^{-1}\):

\[ \mathbf{v}^{\text{goal}}=v_{\text{ref}}\,\frac{\mathbf{q}_{\text{look}}-\mathbf{p}}{\lVert\mathbf{q}_{\text{look}}-\mathbf{p}\rVert}\;-\;k_{\text{lat}}\,(e-e_{\text{lane}})\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}. \]

The second term is a proportional controller: 4 m off the lane line produces a 2 m/s correction towards it.

3.6 Sectors, polygons and restricted areas

The map's bounding box is cut into a 3 × 2 grid of sectors. With column \(c=\lfloor 3(x-x_{\min})/(x_{\max}-x_{\min})\rfloor\) and row \(r=\lfloor 2(y-y_{\min})/(y_{\max}-y_{\min})\rfloor\), the sector id is \(3r+c\in\{0,\dots,5\}\).

A restricted area (TFR) is a polygon. A point is inside if a ray from it crosses the boundary an odd number of times (the even–odd rule). A route segment enters a polygon if it crosses an edge, which is tested with the sign of the cross product \(\operatorname{orient}(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c})=(b_x-a_x)(c_y-a_y)-(b_y-a_y)(c_x-a_x)\).

4.3 The Laplacian and why \(\lambda_2\) matters

The graph Laplacian is \(L=D-A\), where \(D\) is the diagonal matrix of degrees. For the example:

\[ L=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&-1&0\\-1&2&-1&0\\-1&-1&3&-1\\0&0&-1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \text{eigenvalues } 0,\ 1,\ 3,\ 4. \]
  • The smallest eigenvalue is always 0 (eigenvector: all ones), because each row of \(L\) sums to zero.
  • The number of zero eigenvalues equals the number of connected components. So \(\lambda_2\gt0\) exactly when the graph is connected; the code reports \(\lambda_2=0\) for any disconnected graph.
  • For averaging consensus \(\dot{\mathbf{x}}=-L\mathbf{x}\), disagreement decays like \(e^{-\lambda_2 t}\). A larger \(\lambda_2\) means the swarm agrees faster. That is why it is called algebraic connectivity, or the Fiedler value.
ExampleRemove edge 3–4 and drone 4 is isolated: two components, two zero eigenvalues, \(\lambda_2=0\). The Network panel would show "Components 2 (1 isolated)" and \(\lambda_2=0\), while the giant-component value is still computed on drones 1–3.

4.4 The conflict graph and connected components

ADMM uses a second graph: an edge joins two drones on the same layer closer than 20 m. Each connected component of this graph is solved together. Breadth-first search finds components: start from a drone, visit its neighbours, then theirs, until nothing new appears.

In the three-drone example of 1.5, edges (1,2) and (2,3) put all three drones in one component even though drones 1 and 3 are 30 m apart. An earlier version split large components into chunks of five; that could separate two drones that were in conflict and silently drop their constraint (audit finding D05). The fix keeps every component whole.

5Local rules: the decentralised tier

Each drone computes its own desired velocity from what it hears from neighbours.

5.1 Car-following: the time-headway law

A drone keeps a gap to the drone ahead in its lane that grows with speed: \(g\ge d_0+h\,v\). Solving for speed gives the rule

\[ v_{\text{ref}}\le\frac{g-d_0}{h},\qquad d_0=12\ \text{m},\ h=1\ \text{s}. \]
ExampleGap 20 m: \(v\le(20-12)/1=8\) m/s, so a drone cruising at 12 m/s slows to 8. Gap 12 m or less: speed 0, it waits. Gap 30 m: limit 18 m/s, above cruise, so no effect.

The same law gives the layer-agreement "follow penalty" \(\varphi=1-v_{\text{eff}}/v_c\), the fraction of cruise speed lost behind traffic on a layer (Chapter 8).

5.2 Boids: separation, alignment, cohesion

Reynolds' boids rules, as the code implements them:

\[ \mathbf{s}_i=\sum_{j:\,0\lt d_{ij}\lt r_{\text{sep}}}\frac{\mathbf{p}_i-\mathbf{p}_j}{d_{ij}^2},\qquad \mathbf{a}_i=\overline{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathcal{N}}-\mathbf{v}_i,\qquad \mathbf{c}_i=\big(\overline{\mathbf{p}}_{\mathcal{N}}-\mathbf{p}_i\big)\ \text{along the road}. \]
  • Separation pushes away from neighbours closer than \(r_{\text{sep}}=20\) m. Dividing the unit direction by distance once more (\(1/d^2\) overall) makes close neighbours push much harder.
  • Alignment steers towards the average velocity of same-direction neighbours.
  • Cohesion pulls towards the average position, only along the road, and is switched off when any segment mate is closer than \(\max(r_{\text{sep}},\ d_0+hv)\), so it can never pull a drone inside its headway.
ExampleA neighbour 5 m to my right: \(\mathbf{p}_i-\mathbf{p}_j=(0,5)\), \(d=5\), so \(\mathbf{s}=(0,5)/25=(0,0.2)\). With weight \(w_{\text{sep}}=35\) this adds 7 m/s to the left. At 10 m the push is \(35\times0.1=3.5\) m/s; at 20 m it stops.

5.3 Closest point of approach (CPA)

For crossing or head-on traffic, a drone predicts where a neighbour will be if both keep their velocities. With relative position \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{p}_j-\mathbf{p}_i\) and relative velocity \(\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}_j-\mathbf{v}_i\), the distance at time \(\tau\) is \(\lVert\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{u}\tau\rVert\). Setting the derivative of its square to zero gives

\[ \tau^\ast=-\frac{\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u}},\qquad \text{miss}=\lVert\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{u}\,\tau^\ast\rVert. \]
Example\(\mathbf{r}=(40,3)\), my velocity \((10,0)\), theirs \((-10,0)\), so \(\mathbf{u}=(-20,0)\). \(\tau^\ast=-(40\cdot-20)/400=2\) s; miss \(=\lVert(40,3)+(-40,0)\rVert=3\) m. That is below \(1.6\,d_{\min}=12.8\) m, so the drone adds an avoidance velocity away from the miss vector with urgency \(2(12.8-3)/\max(\tau^\ast,0.5)=9.8\), capped at 6 m/s, and slows because the threat is ahead. Because 3 m < 8 m and \(\tau^\ast\lt3\) s, it is also flagged as a threat for a tactical altitude shift.

6Optimisation and consensus ADMM

When drones are about to get too close, they negotiate their next positions by solving a small optimisation problem together.

6.1 The problem each conflict group solves

Every drone \(i\) in a group has its current position \(\mathbf{p}_i\) and an intended next position \(\mathbf{t}_i=\mathbf{p}_i+\mathbf{v}_i\Delta t\). The group picks next positions \(\mathbf{x}_i\) that stay as close as possible to the intentions while keeping apart:

\[ \min_{\{\mathbf{x}_i\}}\ \sum_i\frac{w_i}{2}\lVert\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{t}_i\rVert^2\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \hat{\mathbf{n}}_{ij}\cdot(\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_j)\ge d_s\ \ \forall(i,j),\qquad \lVert\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{p}_i\rVert\le v_{\max}\Delta t. \]

Here \(d_s=1.01\,d_{\min}=8.08\) m and \(v_{\max}\Delta t=1.5\) m. The weights \(w_i\) say who should move less: 10 for emergency, 3 for eVTOL, 1 for delivery.

6.2 Convex sets and why we linearise

A set is convex if the straight line between any two of its points stays inside it. Minimising a convex bowl (sum of squares) over a convex set has one answer and fast algorithms. "Stay at least 8 m apart", \(\lVert\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_j\rVert\ge d_s\), is not convex: it is the outside of a circle. The code replaces it with a half-space, using the current direction between the drones \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{ij}=(\mathbf{p}_i-\mathbf{p}_j)/\lVert\mathbf{p}_i-\mathbf{p}_j\rVert\):

\[ \hat{\mathbf{n}}_{ij}\cdot(\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_j)\ge d_s. \]
Rule · the half-space is safeFor any unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) and any vector \(\mathbf{v}\), Cauchy–Schwarz gives \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\mathbf{v}\le\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert\). So if \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\cdot(\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_j)\ge d_s\), then \(\lVert\mathbf{x}_i-\mathbf{x}_j\rVert\ge d_s\). The linear rule is stricter than the real one, never looser.

Drones on different layers get no constraint at all (their layer index differs by at least 1), and a pair only gets one if it starts within \(d_s+2v_{\max}\Delta t=11.08\) m: farther pairs cannot meet in one tick.

6.4 Consensus ADMM, step by step

ADMM (alternating direction method of multipliers) splits a hard problem into easy pieces that agree with each other. Each drone \(i\) keeps its own decision \(\mathbf{x}_i\). Every constraint \(k\) it takes part in keeps a copy \(\mathbf{z}_{ik}\) of that decision that satisfies the constraint, plus a running disagreement (scaled dual variable) \(\mathbf{u}_{ik}\). \(K_i\) is the number of copies drone \(i\) has: one speed ball plus one per pair constraint. Then repeat:

\[ \begin{aligned} &\textbf{x-update (each drone, closed form):}&& \mathbf{x}_i=\frac{w_i\,\mathbf{t}_i+\rho\sum_k(\mathbf{z}_{ik}-\mathbf{u}_{ik})}{w_i+\rho K_i}\\ &\textbf{z-update (each constraint):}&& \mathbf{z}_k=\operatorname{Proj}_{C_k}\!\big(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u}_k\big)\\ &\textbf{u-update (dual):}&& \mathbf{u}_k\leftarrow\mathbf{u}_k+\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}_k \end{aligned} \]

The x-update is a weighted average of the drone's own intention (weight \(w_i\)) and what its constraints want (weight \(\rho\) each). It comes from setting the gradient of \(\tfrac{w_i}{2}\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{t}_i\rVert^2+\tfrac{\rho}{2}\sum_k\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}_{ik}+\mathbf{u}_{ik}\rVert^2\) to zero. A heavy emergency drone (\(w=10\)) stays near its intention and the others move.

6.5 When to stop: residuals

The primal residual \(r=\big(\sum\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z}\rVert^2\big)^{1/2}\) measures how far decisions are from their rule-satisfying copies. The dual residual \(s=\rho\big(\sum\lVert\mathbf{z}^{\text{new}}-\mathbf{z}^{\text{old}}\rVert^2\big)^{1/2}\) measures whether the copies are still moving. Stop when both are small:

\[ r\le\sqrt{m}\,\varepsilon_{\text{abs}}+\varepsilon_{\text{rel}}\max(\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert,\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert),\qquad s\le\sqrt{m}\,\varepsilon_{\text{abs}}+\varepsilon_{\text{rel}}\,\rho\,\lVert\mathbf{u}\rVert, \]

with \(m=\sum_iK_i\), \(\varepsilon_{\text{abs}}=10^{-3}\), \(\varepsilon_{\text{rel}}=10^{-4}\), \(\rho=1\), at most 50 iterations. If it has not converged after 50, the group keeps its original intended velocities and counts as a fallback. An unverified iterate is never used (audit finding D07).

7Consensus, attacks and W-MSR

Averaging is how a swarm agrees. One liar can break plain averaging; W-MSR stops that.

7.1 Averaging consensus as a matrix

Each honest drone replaces its value by the average of its own and its neighbours' values:

\[ x_i(k+1)=\frac{1}{1+\deg_i}\Big(x_i(k)+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_i}x_j(k)\Big)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathbf{x}(k+1)=W\mathbf{x}(k), \]

where \(W=(I+D)^{-1}(I+A)\). Every row of \(W\) is non-negative and sums to 1 (it is row-stochastic), so each new value lies between the smallest and largest old values. On a connected graph all values converge to one common value.

7.2 What a liar does

A malicious drone that always reports a fixed value \(c\) never updates. Its neighbours keep averaging \(c\) in, and on a connected graph every honest value is dragged to \(c\). In the position-spoofing attack, the liar broadcasts its true position plus an offset (constant, 25–30 m in the presets, or growing over time at a set rate).

7.3 The W-MSR rule

W-MSR (weighted mean-subsequence-reduced) assumes at most \(f\) liars in any neighbourhood. Before averaging, drone \(i\) sorts the reports and removes up to \(f\) values strictly above its own value (the largest ones) and up to \(f\) strictly below (the smallest ones). It averages its own value with what is left.

\[ x_i(k+1)=\operatorname{mean}\Big(\{x_i(k)\}\cup\mathcal{R}_i(k)\Big),\quad \mathcal{R}_i=\text{reports minus the }f\text{ largest above }x_i\text{ and the }f\text{ smallest below }x_i. \]

If there are \(2f\) or fewer reports, the code takes the median of own value and reports instead.

Example · computed with wmsr_scalarOwn value 10, reports 9, 11, 12, 50, \(f=1\). Above 10: \(\{11,12,50\}\), drop the largest one, keep 11 and 12. Below 10: \(\{9\}\), drop it. Rule · when W-MSR is guaranteed to workThe classic result (LeBlanc et al., 2013) needs the graph to be \((2f+1)\)-robust: roughly, every group of drones has some member with at least \(2f+1\) neighbours outside the group. With sparse V2V graphs (the live Network panel often shows average degree near 1) that condition does not hold everywhere, so MASooda reports W-MSR's effect empirically, not as a guarantee.

9Signal temporal logic: measuring safety

STL turns "the drones are always at least 8 m apart" into a number that says how safely the rule holds.

9.1 Signals, predicates and operators

A signal is a value over time, here \(d_{ij}(t)\). A predicate is a test on it, \(d_{ij}(t)\ge d_{\min}\). STL adds time operators:

  • \(\mathbf{G}\,\varphi\) ("globally" or "always"): \(\varphi\) holds at every time in the window;
  • \(\mathbf{F}\,\varphi\) ("finally" or "eventually"): \(\varphi\) holds at some time in the window.

MASooda's safety specification is \(\varphi_{\text{safe}}=\mathbf{G}\,\big(d_{ij}(t)\ge d_{\min}\big)\) for all pairs \(i\ne j\), with \(d_{\min}=8\) m.

9.2 Robustness: how much margin is left

Quantitative semantics give each formula a signed number \(\rho\): positive means satisfied with that much margin, negative means violated by that much. The rules are mechanical: a predicate \(f\ge c\) has robustness \(f-c\); "and" and "always" take the minimum; "or" and "eventually" take the maximum.

\[ \rho(t)=\min_{i\ne j}\big(d_{ij}(t)-d_{\min}\big),\qquad \rho\big(\mathbf{G}\varphi\big)=\min_t\rho(t),\qquad \rho\big(\mathbf{F}\varphi\big)=\max_t\rho(t). \]
ExampleThree snapshots of the closest pair: 16.1 m, 9.0 m, 6.5 m. Then \(\rho=8.1,\ 1.0,\ -1.5\) m and \(\rho(\mathbf{G}\varphi)=-1.5\) m: violated, by 1.5 m, at the third sample.

9.3 Layer-aware distance

Two drones on different layers are not close even if they overlap on the map. The final system measures a 3D distance with the layer gap converted to metres (\(H=15\) m per layer):

\[ d_{ij}=\big\lVert(\Delta x,\ \Delta y,\ \Delta L\cdot H)\big\rVert. \]
ExampleDrones directly above each other on L0 and L1: \(d=\sqrt{0+0+15^2}=15\) m, \(\rho=+7\) m. On the same layer 5 m apart: \(\rho=-3\) m. The earlier XY-only metric (audit D01) would have scored both pairs as violations; that is why the Week 6 violation counts were inflated.

9.4 Reachability: "eventually arrive"

\[ \rho\big(\mathbf{F}(\lVert\mathbf{p}_i-\mathbf{g}_i\rVert\le r)\big)=\min_i\max_t\big(r-\lVert\mathbf{p}_i(t)-\mathbf{g}_i\rVert\big). \]

"Every drone eventually gets within \(r\) of its goal": max over time (eventually), min over drones (for all).

10.2 Hover, induced velocity and forward flight

Rotors push air down. Momentum theory says that to make thrust \(T=mg\) over disc area \(A\), the air through the disc must move at the hover-induced velocity

\[ v_h=\sqrt{\frac{T}{2\rho_aA}}. \]

In forward flight at airspeed \(v\), the rotor meets fresh air and needs less induced velocity:

\[ v_i=\sqrt{-\tfrac{v^2}{2}+\sqrt{\tfrac{v^4}{4}+v_h^4}}. \]

Mechanical power is induced power (divided by the figure of merit FM) plus drag on the body; electrical power divides by propulsion efficiency \(\eta\) and motor health \(h_m\) and adds avionics:

\[ P_{\text{mech}}=\frac{T\,v_i}{\mathrm{FM}}+\tfrac12\rho_aC_DA_f\,v^3\ (+\,T\,v_{\text{climb}}\text{ while climbing}),\qquad P_{\text{el}}=\frac{P_{\text{mech}}}{\eta\,h_m}+P_{\text{av}}. \]

11The battery: a 1-RC equivalent circuit

A lithium pack behaves like a voltage source with resistors and one capacitor. That small circuit predicts voltage sag, heat and remaining energy.

11.1 The circuit

The terminal voltage is \(V=\mathrm{OCV}-V_{RC}-IR_0\). The drone asks for an electrical power \(P\), so \(P=VI=(E-IR_0)I\) with \(E=\mathrm{OCV}-V_{RC}\). That is a quadratic in \(I\):

\[ R_0I^2-EI+P=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad I=\frac{E-\sqrt{E^2-4R_0P}}{2R_0}. \]

The smaller root is the physical one. If \(E^2\lt4R_0P\) there is no real root: the pack cannot deliver that power (the BMS flags it as a current FAULT).

12Estimation: the extended Kalman filter

The BMS cannot see the true charge. It infers it from noisy voltage and current, and it knows how unsure it is.

12.1 Gaussian uncertainty

An estimate is a mean \(\hat{x}\) plus a variance \(\sigma^2\) (or, for several numbers, a covariance matrix \(P\)). For a Gaussian error, the true value lies below \(\hat{x}-z\sigma\) with probability \(1-\Phi(z)\), where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal cumulative distribution. For \(z=1.645\), that probability is 5%.

12.2 The Kalman filter in one dimension

Each step has two halves. Predict with the model, which adds uncertainty \(q\). Update with a measurement of variance \(r\), weighting it by the Kalman gain \(k\):

\[ \text{predict: } \hat{x}^-=f(\hat{x}),\ \ p^-=p+q;\qquad \text{update: } k=\frac{p^-}{p^-+r},\ \ \hat{x}=\hat{x}^-+k\,(y-\hat{y}),\ \ p=(1-k)\,p^-. \]
ExamplePrior SoC estimate 50% with \(p^-=4\) (σ = 2 points). A voltage reading implies 47% with \(r=1\). Then \(k=4/5=0.8\), the new estimate is \(50+0.8(47-50)=47.6\%\), and \(p=0.2\times4=0.8\) (σ ≈ 0.9). The filter trusts whichever source is less uncertain, and the uncertainty shrinks after every measurement.

12.3 MASooda's three-state EKF

The state is \(\mathbf{x}=(\mathrm{SoC},\ V_{RC},\ 1/Q)\). Estimating \(1/Q\) lets the BMS learn a sudden capacity loss from the voltage instead of being told. With measured current \(\tilde{I}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \textbf{predict:}\quad &\widehat{\mathrm{SoC}}^-=\widehat{\mathrm{SoC}}-\tilde{I}\,\Delta t\,\widehat{q}/3600,\qquad \hat{V}_{RC}^-=\hat{V}_{RC}e^{-\Delta t/\tau}+R_1(1-e^{-\Delta t/\tau})\tilde{I},\\ &F=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-\tilde{I}\Delta t/3600\\0&e^{-\Delta t/\tau}&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad P^-=FPF^\top+Q_{\text{proc}},\\[4pt] \textbf{update:}\quad &\hat{V}=n_s\,\mathrm{OCV}(\widehat{\mathrm{SoC}}^-+\bar\delta)-\hat{V}_{RC}^--R_0\tilde{I},\qquad y=V_{\text{meas}}-\hat{V},\\ &H=\big(n_s\,\mathrm{OCV}'(\cdot),\ -1,\ 0\big),\quad S=HP^-H^\top+\sigma_V^2,\quad K=P^-H^\top/S,\\ &\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}^-+K\,y,\qquad P=P^--K\,HP^-. \end{aligned} \]
  • \(F\) is the Jacobian of the prediction: how each new state depends on each old one. Its top-right entry links capacity errors to charge errors.
  • \(H\) is the Jacobian of the voltage model. The OCV slope \(\mathrm{OCV}'\) is computed by a central difference over ±0.5% SoC. Where the OCV curve is flat, voltage tells little about SoC and the gain is small.
  • \(P\) is kept symmetric after every update: \(P\leftarrow\tfrac12(P+P^\top)\).
  • Sensor noise comes from a hash of (seed, drone, sample count), so a drone's measurements do not depend on which other drones fly, and replays are exact.

13Energy decisions under uncertainty

Every "can I make it?" question uses a cautious bound on the BMS estimate, never the estimate itself.

13.1 The chance-constrained energy bound

\[ E^{\text{BMS}}=E^{\text{use}}\Big(\widehat{\mathrm{SoC}}-z\,\sigma_{\mathrm{SoC}},\ \ \frac{1}{\hat{q}+z\,\sigma_q}\Big),\qquad z=1.645. \]

Both the charge and the capacity are taken at their pessimistic one-sided bounds. For a Gaussian error, each bound is too optimistic only about 5% of the time.

Example\(\widehat{\mathrm{SoC}}=40\%\) with σ = 1%: decisions use \(40-1.645=38.4\%\). The 1.6-point haircut is the price of not overestimating. In the paper's low-charge fleet it kept 6.4 more drones on the ground per run instead of letting them take off and divert.

13.2 Energy needed for the rest of the route

\[ E^{\text{need}}(L)=P_{\text{el}}(v_c)\,\frac{L}{v_c}\,(1+\mu)+P_{\text{el}}(0)\,t_{\text{land}},\qquad \mu=0.2,\ t_{\text{land}}=30\ \text{s}. \]
Example1 km left at 10.8 m/s: flight time 92.6 s. Cruise energy \(1060\times92.6/3600=27.3\) Wh, ×1.2 = 32.7 Wh, plus landing hover \(1379\times30/3600=11.5\) Wh: \(E^{\text{need}}=44.2\) Wh.

13.3 The feasibility test

A route is feasible when \(E^{\text{need}}\le E^{\text{BMS}}\) and the weakest cell, at the charge left on arrival, stays above \(3.0+0.05=3.05\) V while carrying the landing hover (before take-off it must also carry a climb). The cell voltage is \(\mathrm{OCV}(s_k)-I\,r_{\text{cell}}(1+\varepsilon_k)\) with \(I\) from the quadratic in 11.1. The voltage test catches a high-resistance cell that would hit cut-off under load long before its charge runs out.

13.4 Pad arbitration by endurance

When several urgent drones want the same landing pad, it goes to the one that can hover the shortest time:

\[ \tau_i=\frac{3600\,E^{\text{BMS}}_i}{P_{\text{el},i}(0)}\ \text{s},\qquad \tau_i\leftarrow\min(\tau_i,120\ \text{s})\ \text{at CRITICAL or FAULT}. \]

The current holder keeps the pad unless a challenger has at least 15 s less endurance, so reservations do not flip-flop. Other arrivals stop 60 m short and loiter at the tube edge.

Example57 Wh left with 1379 W hover: \(\tau=3600\times57/1379=149\) s. A drone at BMS CRITICAL is capped at 120 s, so it wins the pad over this one by 29 s, more than the 15 s swap margin.
