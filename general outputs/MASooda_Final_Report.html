Final Project Report

Multi-Agent System for Hierarchical, Priority-Aware, and Resilient Urban Air Traffic Management — as built, as demonstrated, and as measured

1. Scenario (as built)

A fleet of up to 500 heterogeneous autonomous drones — emergency/medical drones (priority 0), passenger eVTOLs (priority 1) and parcel-delivery drones (priority 2) — shares low-altitude airspace over the IIT Hyderabad campus (Kandi) and Hyderabad city roads. Each drone is a self-interested agent that hears only neighbours within 40 m over vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) links and senses non-cooperative drones within 30 m. The system runs live at masooda.onrender.com.

The proposal imagined two or three straight corridors. The final airspace follows the real road network, because that is where drone corridors would actually be laid:

  • IITH campus map: 28 roads traced from the campus map (~0.95 m/px) form a graph of 105 junctions with 13 vertiports — V1 Hospital, three depots (Mess A, Mess B, Sampoorna) and nine hubs (Main Gate, Guest House, Convention Centre, LHC, Admin Block, Academic Area, New Hostels, Old Hostels, SNCC) — enclosed by a geofence.
  • Hyderabad map: OpenStreetMap major roads (motorway to tertiary) snapped into a routable graph of 1,533 junctions with 8 vertiports chosen by farthest-point sampling (hub, hospital, depot roles).
  • Corridor tubes: each road is a 20 m tube with one keep-left lane per direction; a drone that drifts more than 10 m off the centreline is projected back onto the tube wall, and the map edge acts as a geofence.
  • 2.5D layered airspace: seven altitude layers L0–L6 stacked 15 m apart from 30 m to 120 m above ground (inside India's 400 ft green-zone ceiling). Following the semicircular rule, eastbound traffic uses even layers (L0, L2, L4), westbound odd layers (L1, L3, L5), and L6 is reserved for medical emergencies.
  • Sectors: the airspace is split into six sectors (3 × 2 grid); every (sector, layer) cell has a nominal capacity of 15 drones.

The system must stay correct when agents behave adversarially (GPS-spoofed position broadcasts, a drone with no Remote ID), when the controller link drops, and when the physical world intervenes: battery faults (capacity loss, cell failure, overheating, motor and GNSS degradation), heat (thinner air at 35–44 °C raises hover power), wind, moving storm cells and pop-up restricted areas.

2. Objective — and What Was Achieved

All five design objectives of the proposal are implemented and run together, every tick (Δt = 0.1 s), in one simulation loop:

  1. Local governance: each drone follows its route by pure pursuit (look-ahead 40 m), keeps a car-following gap v ≤ (g − d₀)/h with d₀ = 12 m and h = 1 s, applies boids separation and alignment, and avoids neighbours by closest point of approach. Conflicting groups (same layer, within 20 m) negotiate their next positions with consensus ADMM; a crowded drone can also make a tactical altitude shift.
  2. Global governance: the Drone Traffic Controller (DTC) runs every 2 s per sector: it counts (sector, layer) load, keeps emergencies on L6, moves drones out of over-capacity cells through a safety gate (a layer change is deferred if the target layer is occupied nearby), seeds layer congestion prices and executes operator commands. If the link fails, drones follow their lost-link policy (continue, hold, return to base or land) and operator commands wait in a queue for up to 30 s.
  3. Priority arbitration: emergency > eVTOL > delivery is respected globally (reserved layer, priority-aware allocation) and locally (ADMM weights 10 / 3 / 1, and the lower-priority drone yields in a tactical shift). A drone whose battery management system (BMS) reports CRITICAL or FAULT is treated as priority 0 for right of way to land, and its landing pad is reserved.
  4. Adversarial resilience: every received position report passes a trusted-range check (a report that disagrees with the sensed range by more than 3 m is moved along its bearing to that range) and W-MSR fusion with f = 1; the operator can also quarantine a drone so its reports are ignored network-wide.
  5. Formal safety verification: the STL specification φ = G(dij(t) ≥ dmin), dmin = 8 m, is monitored live through its robustness ρ(t) = mini≠j dij(t) − dmin, using a layer-aware 3D distance (horizontal offset plus layer gap × layer height), independent of which tier acted. A reachability spec F(reach destination) is also implemented.

Underlying research question:

Can a two-tier (decentralized-local / centralized-global) multi-agent architecture achieve scalable, priority-aware, and formally verifiable safe traffic management in dense UAM airspace, while remaining robust to adversarial or faulty agents — outperforming a purely centralized or purely decentralized alternative under the same conditions?

What our evaluation found (100 drones on the campus map, 20 paired seeds):

Hierarchical and decentralized control both delivered 95.7% of flights with zero near misses and a 12.5 m minimum separation; the centralized controller, acting on reports refreshed at its update rate, had 9.8 near misses and 1.1 collisions per run and delivered 88.0%. The hierarchy is as safe as decentralized control and clearly safer than central control. In nominal traffic its DTC did not add measurable safety on top of the local tier — its value shows in the scenarios it alone can handle: emergencies, ground stops, restricted areas, battery faults and operator commands.

4. How One Simulation Tick Works

Every module the team built runs inside the same loop (simulation/route_engine.py), in this order:

  1. Departures: queued drones leave their pad when the pad airspace is clear and the corridor junction has a merge gap; otherwise they are held on the ground (logged, e.g. "ground hold at V10: pad airspace occupied").
  2. Neighbour search: V2V graph within 40 m (spatial grid index, so 500 drones stay fast); non-cooperative drones are visible only to onboard sensing within 30 m.
  3. Attack and defence: spoofers falsify their broadcasts; receivers apply the trusted-range check and W-MSR before using any report.
  4. Local behaviour: route following, car-following, boids, closest-point-of-approach avoidance, junction merging and platoon tracking.
  5. ADMM: conflict groups on the same layer within 20 m solve for their next positions; groups that do not converge in 50 iterations keep their intended velocities and count as fallbacks.
  6. Dynamics and boundaries: velocity and acceleration limits (15 m/s), tube and geofence projection, crash detection (< 2 m).
  7. Battery and health (every 0.5–1 s): power, pack and cell model, BMS estimate, feasibility checks, onboard responses.
  8. DTC (every 2 s) and layer agreement (every 1 s): sector × layer allocation, emergency layer, pad arbitration, congestion prices, operator commands.
  9. Verification: layer-aware STL robustness, near-miss episodes, KPIs; a compact frame streams to the browser.

2) What We Promised vs. What We Delivered

Committed publicly (our "promise") — all delivered:

  • 2D (or 2.5D dual-panel) simulation → 2D map + 2.5D side view, seven altitude layers
  • 50–80 agents, 2–3 corridors → up to 500 drones on full road networks (105-junction campus, 1,533-junction city)
  • One local rule set + one global overseer with priority arbitration → boids, car-following and CPA avoidance; a sector-based DTC with a reserved emergency layer
  • One resilient consensus defense against one attack type → trusted-range check + W-MSR against position spoofing, plus operator quarantine
  • One STL spec monitored live + one comparison chart → live layer-aware ρ(t); four architectures from identical initial conditions

Headroom and stretch items:

  • Second STL spec (reachability) → implemented · corridor congestion pricing → implemented (layer agreement with consensus prices) · richer priority-conflict interface → implemented (DTC console)
  • 500+ agents → reached: 500 drones at 3.4× real time on a 2-vCPU server · multiple attack types → partly: spoofing and a non-cooperative intruder
  • True 3D rendering, full epistemic-logic belief modelling, learned (RL) policies → not done, as the proposal allowed

Grown beyond the proposal during weeks 6–8:

  • Drones carry a physical battery and BMS, and the traffic manager uses the BMS state (priority to land, pad arbitration, energy-weighted ADMM).
  • Weather (temperature, air density, wind), moving storm cells, temporary flight restrictions and ground-delay programs.
  • A deployed web demo with an operator console, ten scripted scenarios and run export / replay; an IEEE-style paper draft with 460 paired-seed runs.

6) Testing, Reproducibility & Limitations

  • Tests: 421 test functions in 80 files — unit tests per module (ADMM solver, boids, overseer, attack, W-MSR, STL), route-engine tests that assert every airborne drone stays inside its tube and the geofence on every tick, grid-on vs. grid-off identity, energy balance to 10−9, estimator calibration, pad arbitration, DTC commands, presets, KPI definitions and streaming.
  • Reproducibility: one config file (configs/base_config.yaml) for every parameter; a seed fixes a run exactly; an initial-conditions hash proves architectures start identically; scripts/run_all.py regenerates Week 6; 53 runs repeated on a second platform gave identical discrete outcomes.
  • Limitations: the battery, power and thermal models are not calibrated on flight data; health telemetry is trusted (a drone falsely reporting CRITICAL would get right of way); ADMM is a mitigation layer, not a formal guarantee; the threshold thermal response is too slow at 44 °C; wind changes power but does not push drones; V2V message loss is not modelled; Week 6 results are provisional.
  • Next steps: calibrate against public delivery-drone flight data; predictive thermal and energy STL specs such as G(Tp ≤ Tmax); protect health reports the way W-MSR protects positions; scenarios with scarce landing capacity.
